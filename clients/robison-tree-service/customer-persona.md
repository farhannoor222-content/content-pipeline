## Robison Tree Service Customer Profile - Detailed Breakdown

## Overview of the Typical Client

The typical Robison Tree Service client is a homeowner who has lived in their property for 5-15 years. They've reached a stage where the trees on their property have grown significantly or started showing signs of age-related issues. These clients are typically homeowners rather than renters, with 85% owning single-family homes on lots ranging from 0.25 to 2 acres. They tend to be financially stable, willing to invest in their property, and value expertise over the lowest price. Many discovered tree issues during seasonal changes or after weather events. They typically conduct research before hiring, checking 3-4 service providers, and strongly prefer companies with proper credentials and insurance. Most aren't knowledgeable about arboriculture but recognize when professional help is needed.

* Location: Primarily Lexington neighborhoods (Chevy Chase, Hartland, Andover, Hamburg, Lansdowne, Beaumont), with growing client bases in Georgetown, Versailles, Nicholasville, and Winchester
* Occupation: Healthcare professionals (22%), educators (18%), business professionals (17%), government employees (12%), technology sector (10%), retirees (15%), and various others (6%)

## Common Trees in Lexington Area

* Oak Varieties:
   * White Oak (Quercus alba): Abundant, long-lived, 60-100 ft mature height, susceptible to oak wilt
   * Northern Red Oak (Quercus rubra): Fast-growing, common in residential areas, vivid fall color
   * Pin Oak (Quercus palustris): Popular landscape tree, distinctive branch architecture, chlorosis issues in alkaline soils
   * Scarlet Oak (Quercus coccinea): Brilliant fall color, less common but valued specimen
   * Bur Oak (Quercus macrocarpa): Drought-resistant, large spreading canopy, long-lived native
* Maple Varieties:
   * Sugar Maple (Acer saccharum): Excellent fall color, native, susceptible to urban stresses
   * Red Maple (Acer rubrum): Adaptable, common landscape tree, early spring color
   * Silver Maple (Acer saccharinum): Fast-growing, weak-wooded, common in older neighborhoods
   * Norway Maple (Acer platanoides): Non-native, invasive tendencies, common in older developments
* Other Deciduous Trees:
   * American Ash (Fraxinus americana): Declining due to Emerald Ash Borer infestation, requires preventative treatment
   * Eastern Redbud (Cercis canadensis): Native flowering tree, understory species, heart-shaped leaves
   * Flowering Dogwood (Cornus florida): Native ornamental, susceptible to anthracnose
   * Tulip Poplar (Liriodendron tulipifera): Kentucky state tree, tall and fast-growing
   * American Sycamore (Platanus occidentalis): Large native tree, distinctive exfoliating bark
   * Sweetgum (Liquidambar styraciflua): Distinctive star-shaped leaves, problematic seed balls
   * River Birch (Betula nigra): Multi-stemmed, attractive exfoliating bark, popular landscape choice
   * Honey Locust (Gleditsia triacanthos): Thornless varieties common in landscapes, filtered shade
   * Black Walnut (Juglans nigra): Native timber tree, allelopathic properties affect nearby plants
   * Kentucky Coffeetree (Gymnocladus dioicus): Native, pollution tolerant, interesting winter form
* Coniferous Trees:
   * Eastern White Pine (Pinus strobus): Soft-needled, susceptible to white pine weevil
   * Eastern Red Cedar (Juniperus virginiana): Drought-tolerant native, cedar-apple rust host
   * Norway Spruce (Picea abies): Common landscape conifer, heat stress in urban settings
   * Colorado Blue Spruce (Picea pungens): Popular ornamental, susceptible to needle cast diseases
   * Arborvitae (Thuja occidentalis): Common for screening, deer damage issues
* Problem Trees in the Region:
   * Bradford Pear (Pyrus calleryana 'Bradford'): Weak structure, invasive, short-lived
   * White Ash (affected by Emerald Ash Borer): Significant decline throughout region
   * Silver Maple: Weak wood, aggressive roots, damage-prone
   * Tree of Heaven (Ailanthus altissima): Invasive, weak-wooded, prolific spread
   * Boxelder (Acer negundo): Weak-wooded, short-lived, boxelder bug issues
* Regional Tree Challenges:
   * Emerald Ash Borer: Devastating ash populations throughout the region
   * Oak wilt: Spreading through oak populations
   * Bacterial leaf scorch: Affecting oak species
   * Japanese beetles: Defoliating various species
   * Bagworms: Common on conifers
   * Dutch elm disease: Limited remaining elms
   * Ice and storm damage: Common winter and spring issue
   * Soil compaction in urban settings: Limiting root development
   * Improper planting depth: Leading to girdling roots
   * Excessive mulching ("volcano mulching"): Causing bark damage and disease
