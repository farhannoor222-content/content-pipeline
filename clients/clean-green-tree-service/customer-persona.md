# Clean Green Tree Service Customer Profile

## Overview of the Typical Client

The typical Clean Green Tree Service client is a property owner who values their trees but doesn't have specialized knowledge to properly maintain them. These individuals recognize trees as important assets that contribute to property value, shade, and aesthetics. They typically contact tree services when they notice visible problems or after weather events that cause damage. They want professional help but often feel uncertain about what exactly needs to be done.

### Most clients fall into one of these categories

* Established homeowners with mature landscaping (10+ years in home)
* New homeowners discovering tree issues after purchase
* Commercial property owners/managers concerned about appearance and liability
* Elderly homeowners unable to monitor or maintain trees themselves
* Homeowners preparing to sell their property who need to address tree issues

The typical client has owned their property for 5+ years and has at least 3-4 significant trees requiring professional attention.

## Demographic Example (a fictional persona)

Eleanor Rodriguez: Eleanor is a 67-year-old retired school administrator who has lived in her ranch-style home in southwest Champaign for over 30 years. Widowed for five years, she lives alone and manages on a comfortable retirement income of $78,000 annually from her pension and investments.

Her property includes several aging trees planted when her subdivision was built in the 1970s – two large silver maples, a struggling ash tree affected by emerald ash borer, and an ornamental crabapple she and her late husband planted on their 25th anniversary.

Eleanor has limited physical ability to inspect her trees or handle yard work herself. She's budget-conscious but willing to pay for necessary services to maintain her home where she hopes to age in place. She has a strong emotional attachment to her trees, especially those planted with her husband.

Her initial contact with Clean Green Tree Service came after she noticed concerning leaf spots on her maple and significant dieback on the upper branches. She gathered information from her garden club friends and neighbors before calling for help.

Eleanor's primary concerns are preserving her trees if possible, finding affordable solutions, and working with trustworthy service providers who won't take advantage of her limited knowledge. She values clear explanations in non-technical language and appreciates companies that respect her property and clean up thoroughly.

## Decision-Making Process

### Discovery Phase

* Initial trigger events:
   * Visual cues (dead branches, leaf discoloration, fungal growth)
   * Environmental events (storm damage, fallen limbs)
   * Seasonal observations (spring leaf-out issues, fall preparation)
   * Neighbor or friend comments about tree condition
   * Real estate transaction requirements
* Awareness timeline:
   * Emergency situations: Immediate action (within 24-48 hours)
   * Visible problems: 1-3 weeks consideration
   * Preventative maintenance: 2-4 weeks research phase
* Information gathering:
   * 78% consult friends/neighbors for recommendations
   * 65% conduct online searches for tree services
   * 52% check review sites (Google, Facebook, Yelp)
   * 38% drive by properties where they've seen tree work

### Evaluation Phase

* Number of companies contacted:
   * Emergency situations: 1-2 companies
   * Standard service: 2-3 companies
   * Large projects ($1,000+): Often 3+ quotes
* Evaluation criteria:
   * Response time to initial contact (expected within 24 business hours)
   * Professionalism during assessment (uniformed, knowledgeable)
   * Clarity of explanation (non-technical terms, visual aids)
   * Written estimate detail (itemized services, timeline)
   * Credentials and insurance verification (expect proof)
   * Previous work examples (photos, references)
* Consultation expectations:
   * Scheduled appointments kept punctually
   * Thorough property examination (not just problem areas)
   * Multiple options presented when applicable
   * Educational approach vs. sales pressure
   * Questions answered patiently and completely

### Selection Phase

* Key decision factors:
   * Perceived expertise and trustworthiness (highest factor)
   * Value perception (not necessarily lowest price)
   * Availability and scheduling flexibility
   * Guarantee/warranty offerings
   * Payment terms and options
* Decision timing:
   * Emergency work: Same-day decisions common
   * Standard service: 2-7 days after final quote
   * Large projects: 1-2 weeks of consideration
* Decision authority:
   * Residential: Typically joint decision in households
   * Commercial: Often requires multiple approvals
   * Price threshold for extended consideration: ~$800
* Contract expectations:
   * Clear scope of work documentation
   * Pricing transparency and guarantee
   * Timeline commitments
   * Property protection specifications
   * Cleanup standards defined

### Post-Decision Phase

* Scheduling expectations:
   * Emergency response: Same day to 48 hours
   * Standard work: Within 2 weeks of approval
   * Seasonal flexibility for non-urgent work
   * Communication about weather delays
* Preparation expectations:
   * Clear instructions about access needs
   * Advance notice about utilities/structures
   * Guidance on property preparation

## Possible Objections

1. "It's too expensive"
* Root Concern: Value perception, budget constraints
* Frequency: Very common (70% of hesitant customers)
* Underlying Psychology:
   * Uncertainty about necessary vs. optional services
   * Comparison to DIY misconceptions
   * No budget allocation for unexpected tree care
   * Unclear understanding of long-term value
   * Comparing to inadequate low-cost providers
* Best Response Approaches:
   * Cost comparison to potential property damage
   * Explanation of long-term savings through prevention
   * Tiered service options with clear differences
   * Payment plans for larger projects
   * Insurance discount possibilities
   * Visual evidence of necessity (photos, samples)
2. "The tree looks fine to me"
* Root Concern: Knowledge gap about early disease/structural signs
* Frequency: Common with preventative recommendations
* Underlying Psychology:
   * Limited understanding of progressive tree problems
   * Visual assessment limitations of non-professionals
   * Conflating leafing with overall health
   * Unfamiliarity with species-specific issues
   * Resistance to addressing "invisible" problems
* Best Response Approaches:
   * Visual evidence of hidden problems
   * Education about disease progression timelines
   * Before/after examples from similar situations
   * Simple explanations of biological processes
   * Staged treatment options for monitoring
   * Risk assessment in everyday terms
3. "I need to get more quotes first"
* Root Concern: Price verification, commitment avoidance

## Types of Trees in that Area

1. Native Deciduous Trees
* Oak Species:
   * White Oak (Quercus alba): Long-lived, broad specimens common in older neighborhoods
   * Red Oak (Quercus rubra): Faster growing, found in developments from 1960s-1990s
   * Bur Oak (Quercus macrocarpa): Massive specimens in historic districts
   * Pin Oak (Quercus palustris): Common in wetter areas and newer developments
   * Common issues: Oak wilt, bacterial leaf scorch, gall-forming insects
   * Maintenance needs: Structural pruning when young, deadwood removal in maturity
* Maple Species:
   * Silver Maple (Acer saccharinum): Fast-growing, weak-wooded, common in post-war neighborhoods
   * Sugar Maple (Acer saccharum): Slower growing, fall color specimen trees
   * Red Maple (Acer rubrum): Increasingly common in newer developments
   * Common issues: Verticillium wilt, leaf scorch, gloomy scale, weak branch attachments
   * Maintenance needs: Regular structural pruning, frequent deadwood management
* Other Significant Natives:
   * American Elm (Ulmus americana): Surviving specimens or resistant varieties
   * Ash (Fraxinus spp.): Declining due to Emerald Ash Borer infestation
   * Hickory (Carya spp.): Found in older properties and natural areas
   * Sycamore (Platanus occidentalis): Large specimens near water features
   * River Birch (Betula nigra): Popular multi-stemmed landscape tree
   * Common issues: Species-specific diseases, storm damage susceptibility
   * Maintenance needs: Regular monitoring, preventative pruning
2. Ornamental and Landscape Trees
* Flowering Specimen Trees:
   * Dogwood (Cornus florida): Understory tree with spring flowers
   * Redbud (Cercis canadensis): Native understory tree with purple spring blooms
   * Crabapple (Malus spp.): Common in landscapes, multiple varieties
   * Japanese Maple (Acer palmatum): High-value specimen trees in protected locations
   * Serviceberry (Amelanchier spp.): Multi-season interest, becoming more popular
   * Common issues: Apple scab (crabapple), anthracnose (dogwood), environmental stress
   * Maintenance needs: Light pruning, disease monitoring, structure development
* Evergreen Landscape Trees:
   * White Pine (Pinus strobus): Tall specimens in older properties
   * Norway Spruce (Picea abies): Common windbreak and specimen tree
   * Colorado Blue Spruce (Picea pungens): Distinctive color, often in front yards
   * Arborvitae (Thuja occidentalis): Common for screening, various cultivars
   * Juniper (Juniperus spp.): Drought-tolerant landscapes, various forms
   * Common issues: Needle cast diseases, bagworms, winter burn, drought stress
   * Maintenance needs: Minimal pruning, pest monitoring, mulching
* Non-Native Deciduous Trees:
   * Bradford/Callery Pear (Pyrus calleryana): Common in commercial landscapes
   * Norway Maple (Acer platanoides): Found in older neighborhoods
   * Ginkgo (Ginkgo biloba): Street tree and specimen plantings
   * London Planetree (Platanus x acerifolia): Urban-tolerant large shade tree
   * Honey Locust (Gleditsia triacanthos): Common in newer developments
   * Common issues: Structural weaknesses, invasive potential, pest susceptibility
   * Maintenance needs: Structural pruning, containment, monitoring
3. Common Tree Problems in the Area
* Regional Disease Challenges:
   * Emerald Ash Borer: Devastating ash populations throughout region
   * Oak Wilt: Spreading threat to valuable oak specimens
   * Dutch Elm Disease: Continuing threat to surviving elms
   * Apple Scab: Affecting crabapples throughout the area
   * Verticillium Wilt: Affecting maples and other species
   * Management approaches: Preventative treatments, proper pruning timing, monitoring
* Environmental Stress Factors:
   * Urban soil compaction: Common in older neighborhoods
   * Construction damage: Prevalent in developing areas
   * Road salt exposure: Street trees and roadside specimens
   * Drought cycles: Increasingly common with climate patterns
   * Heat stress: Urban heat island effects in developed areas
   * Management approaches: Mulching, proper watering, soil management
* Weather-Related Challenges:
   * Severe thunderstorm damage: Frequent summer occurrence
   * Ice storm loading: Winter branch failures
   * Wind shear: Tornado-prone region with sudden gusts
   * Lightning strikes: Tall trees in open areas
